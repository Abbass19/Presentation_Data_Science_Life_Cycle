# 📘 FINAL CHOSEN 5 SOLUTIONS (THE ONES YOU MUST TEST)

## 1. Logistic Regression Baseline

Logistic Regression serves as the foundational baseline model for all classification tasks in this project. It performs exceptionally well with small datasets, is computationally lightweight, and provides stable, interpretable decision boundaries. Combined with high-quality sentence embeddings, logistic regression often delivers surprisingly competitive results without requiring complex architectures or GPU acceleration. It also establishes a reliable reference point against which all more advanced models can be compared. Because of its speed, simplicity, and strong performance on well-separated classes such as HCAT Domain and Category, it remains the primary benchmark method in this pipeline.

---

## 2. Stacked Sequential Models

Stacked Sequential Models capture relationships between labels by using the predictions (and confidence scores) of earlier models as features for later ones. This is especially powerful for HCAT-style tasks, where many labels—such as Category, Harm, and Severity—are not independent but logically depend on earlier decisions like Domain or Category. By feeding predicted outputs forward in a structured pipeline, these models can learn the same hierarchical decision flow that human coders use. This approach improves accuracy in difficult labels, enhances consistency across outputs, and compensates for limited dataset size. In practice, stacked models provide one of the highest performance boosts with very little additional computation.

---

## 3. XGBoost with Injected Features

XGBoost is a gradient boosting algorithm that excels at learning nonlinear patterns from mixed numeric inputs. When combined with injected features—such as sentence embeddings, predicted label probabilities from earlier models, text length, or issuing department—it becomes a powerful alternative to linear models. XGBoost is particularly effective for small to medium datasets because it handles noise well, automatically captures interaction effects, and does not require heavy hyperparameter tuning. For noisy or fine-grained tasks like Severity, Harm, or Stage of Care, XGBoost
often outperforms both logistic regression and random forest models due to its ability to model subtle dependencies in the data.

---

## 4. Ordinal Logistic Regression (Severity Only)

Severity in HCAT is inherently an ordinal variable, where the classes have a natural order (e.g., 0 < 1 < 2 < 3). Treating this as a normal categorical classification problem ignores this ordering and penalizes the model incorrectly. Ordinal Logistic Regression directly models this ordered relationship, resulting in more meaningful predictions and sharper error control—misclassifying a 3 as 2 is penalized far less than misclassifying a 3 as 0. This aligns closely with the way human coders think about severity and yields improved performance with minimal implementation effort. Because severity is the most context-dependent and difficult label to predict, using an ordinal-aware model provides a mathematically correct and practically advantageous solution.

---

## 5. Better Embeddings (mpnet / AraBERT-SBERT)

High-quality sentence embeddings are essential for reliable text classification, especially when models must operate on small datasets. Replacing standard contextual models like AraBERT with dedicated sentence-transformer models such as “paraphrase-multilingual-mpnet-base-v2” or Arabic Sentence-BERT dramatically improves the semantic representation of complaint narratives. These embedding models are optimized for meaning-level similarity rather than token-level context, producing dense features that downstream classifiers can learn from effectively. They also require no fine-tuning and run efficiently on CPU. For tasks involving Arabic-English mixed text, complex medical terminology, or long narrative descriptions, upgraded embeddings provide substantial performance gains with extremely low engineering cost.




# 📘 Abandoned Solution Classes (High Mathematical Complexity)

These approaches were considered during the design phase but intentionally excluded due to their mathematical complexity, engineering cost, or unsuitability for a dataset of ~500 records. They remain valuable long-term research directions but are not practical for immediate implementation.

---

## A. Parallel Multi-Head Neural Models

Parallel multi-head neural models were rejected because they treat all labels as independent outputs, ignoring the critical dependencies that exist between Domain, Category, Severity, Harm, and Stage. Although multi-head transformers share a common encoder, their individual output heads operate independently, which prevents them from learning the hierarchical relationships built into HCAT. Training and maintaining such models also requires significantly more data and GPU resources than are available. In practice, these architectures underperform compared to sequential pipelines and are not efficient for small datasets or CPU-bound environments.

---

## B. Full Probabilistic Graphical Models (Bayesian Networks)

Probabilistic graphical models, such as Bayesian Networks, offer a theoretically elegant way to model conditional dependencies between HCAT labels. However, constructing and calibrating a full Bayesian network requires substantial statistical expertise, careful structure design, and large amounts of training data to estimate reliable conditional probabilities. With only 500 samples and highly imbalanced subcategories, the learned parameters would be unstable and prone to overfitting. Additionally, integrating soft evidence from text models adds computational overhead and increases implementation complexity. For these reasons, full PGMs were deferred in favor of simpler stacked models and CPT-based heuristics that capture most of the benefits with a fraction of the effort.

---

## C. Full Conditional Random Fields (CRF / Structured Prediction)

Structured prediction models like Conditional Random Fields were evaluated as a theoretical option for capturing cross-label dependencies. While CRFs excel at sequence labeling tasks where outputs form a chain or graph, their effectiveness relies on hand-crafted potential functions and sufficient data to estimate joint distributions. Implementing a CRF on top of text embeddings for nine interdependent labels would require significant mathematical formulation, custom feature engineering, and specialized optimization routines. Given the limited dataset size and the complexity of the label space, a CRF-based system was determined to be excessively complex and unlikely to outperform simpler hierarchical models.
